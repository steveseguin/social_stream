OBS Studio: the basics

Build your first scene, get clear audio, and make a recording before you go live.

Guides / OBS Studio basics

OBS Studio combines your camera, screen, sound, and overlays into a video that you can record or stream. Start with one scene and a microphone; add extras after your first recording works.

1. Install and run the wizard

  1. Download OBS Studio from obsproject.com for Windows, macOS, or Linux, install it, and open it.
  2. Run Tools → Auto-Configuration Wizard. Choose streaming if you intend to go live, or recording if you only want video files.
  3. Follow the prompts and apply the suggested settings. For a modest computer, 1280 × 720 at 30 frames per second is a useful first target.
  4. Allow microphone, camera, and screen capture access when your operating system asks for it. On macOS, check these permissions in System Settings if a source is blank.

2. Build a scene and learn the controls

A scene is a layout, such as “Camera,” “Gameplay,” or “Be right back.” A source is one item inside it: a camera, game, image, microphone, or web page.

  1. In Scenes, press + and name your first scene.
  2. In Sources, press +, select a source type, give it a name, and select its device or window.
  3. Drag its corners in the preview to resize it. Right-click it and use Transform → Fit to Screen for a full-frame source.
  4. Drag items up or down in the Sources list: items at the top cover items below. Put chat and your camera above a full-screen game. Use the eye to hide an item and the lock to prevent accidental movement.

Controls: Start Recording saves locally; Start Streaming sends to your platform. In normal mode, selecting a scene changes the output immediately. Studio Mode gives you a Preview and a Program view; press Transition to send the prepared scene to Program. Shared source properties and audio changes can still affect the live output.

For a second scene, choose Add Existing when reusing a camera or overlay. Changes to that shared source affect every scene using it. More: OBS interface overview.

3. Choose resolution and frame rate

Open Settings → Video. Base (Canvas) Resolution is the space you arrange sources in. Output (Scaled) Resolution is the video size you send or record.

  • Simple landscape setup: use a 1920 × 1080 canvas. Output at 1280 × 720 for a lighter stream, or 1920 × 1080 for more detail.
  • 30 FPS: a good starting point for talking, lessons, and ordinary screen sharing. 60 FPS: smoother fast motion, with more processing and bitrate needed.
  • Portrait: a 1080 × 1920 canvas is vertical. Rearrange your scene to fit it; changing the numbers alone does not create a useful vertical composition.

Use Output (Scaled) Resolution to reduce a stream’s size without rearranging the canvas. Leave additional output rescaling off unless you deliberately need a separate size. Keep standard SDR color settings for a first stream.

4. Use a hardware encoder to reduce CPU load

An encoder compresses your finished scene into video. In Settings → Output, choose a hardware H.264 encoder when available. It moves much of the encoding work from the CPU to dedicated hardware; OBS still needs resources to draw the scene.

Optional: presets, AV1, and HEVC

Start with the wizard’s preset. On NVENC, P5 is a reasonable quality starting point; a lower-numbered preset reduces work if encoding falls behind. Other encoders use different names, so do not copy NVENC options into an AMD or Intel setup.

H.264, HEVC (H.265), and AV1 are video formats; NVENC, QSV, and Apple VT are encoder implementations. AV1 and HEVC can give better quality at a given bitrate, but your hardware and destination must support them. YouTube accepts both; Kick’s standard ingest uses H.264. For local recording, also check that your editor can read the chosen format. See OBS video format guidance.

5. Connect your platform and set the bitrate

Open Settings → Stream, choose the service, and connect your account where offered. Otherwise, copy the server URL and stream key from your platform’s creator dashboard into OBS. Keep the key private; reset it in that dashboard if it is exposed.

Bitrate is the amount of data sent each second. OBS uses kbps: 6,000 kbps is about 6 Mbps of video, plus audio and network overhead. Measure your upload speed and leave room for other traffic. As a practical starting margin, keep the total stream below roughly 70% of a stable measured upload speed.

For manual settings, choose Settings → Output → Output Mode: Advanced → Streaming. Use CBR (constant bitrate), a 2-second keyframe interval, and AAC stereo audio for the setups below. Set resolution and FPS in the Video tab.

Other platforms: use the encoder settings provided in their live dashboard. For Facebook or TikTok, first confirm that your account offers an external encoder connection. A chat connection in SSN does not grant permission to broadcast video to that service.

Optional: streaming to several platforms

A relay service can take one upload and distribute it, while a multi-output setup can upload separately to each destination. Separate uploads add together: two 6 Mbps video streams need more than 12 Mbps once audio and overhead are included. Each destination still needs a compatible format and bitrate; use transcoding or separate encodes when requirements differ.

6. Capture and mix your audio

Start with headphones and one microphone. In Settings → Audio, select the actual microphone under Mic/Auxiliary Audio. Alternatively, add it as an Audio Input Capture source and disable the global copy so you capture it only once.

Choose a computer-audio approach:

  • Whole-device audio: Desktop Audio captures what plays through the selected device, including notifications. Choose the output device you really use.
  • Separate apps on Windows: use Application Audio Capture, or the audio option in supported Game/Window Capture sources. Disable overlapping Desktop Audio capture to prevent duplicates. See the OBS application audio guide.
  • macOS: on macOS 13 or later, current OBS versions offer macOS audio/screen capture for system or application sound. Older setups need a different route; follow the macOS desktop audio guide.

As a starting point, aim for normal speech peaks around −12 to −6 dBFS on the meter, with room for louder speech. Avoid hitting 0 dBFS, which clips. A slider reading of 0 dB means no volume adjustment; it does not mean the signal is clipping.

Lower game, music, and alert levels until speech is easy to understand. Listen to a short recording on ordinary headphones; meters alone do not tell you whether the voice is clear. Start with the microphone’s input gain if it is already distorted. More: OBS audio mixer guide.

Optional: clean up the microphone

Open the microphone’s Filters from its mixer menu. Add a little noise suppression for steady fan noise, an expander to reduce quiet background sound, a compressor to tame loud speech, and a limiter last to catch peaks. Add one at a time and listen. Heavy suppression can distort speech, and a tight gate can cut off words.

If a mono microphone plays only on one side, use Mono in Advanced Audio Properties. For lip sync, record a clap and adjust Sync Offset only after identifying which signal arrives first. See the OBS filter guides.

7. Hear audio without creating an echo

Output sends sound to your stream or recording. Monitoring lets you hear a source through a selected listening device. A moving meter does not prove that either destination is receiving it.

  1. In Settings → Audio → Advanced → Monitoring Device, choose your headphones explicitly.
  2. Open Advanced Audio Properties from the Audio Mixer menu or the Edit menu.
  3. Set each source’s Audio Monitoring mode using the table below.
How each source is routed
ModeOBS sends to headphonesOBS sends directly to output
Monitor OffNoYes
Monitor Only (mute output)YesNo
Monitor and OutputYesYes

The table assumes the source is unmuted and assigned to the output’s audio track. Monitor Off does not mute the stream. It is usually right for your microphone and games you already hear. Use Monitor and Output for an alert or browser source that both you and the audience should hear.

Avoid capturing the monitor return. If Desktop Audio captures the same device used for monitoring, it can record the sound again, causing doubling or a feedback loop. Use separate application capture, or monitor through a device that OBS is not capturing. Monitor Only can still reach viewers indirectly through that second capture.

Keep the platform’s live player muted on your streaming computer. Headphones prevent speaker sound from returning through the microphone, but they do not fix duplicate software capture. For your own voice, a microphone or audio interface’s direct monitoring can avoid OBS monitoring delay.

8. Add browser sources and SSN overlays

A Browser Source draws a web page inside your scene. It can show chat, alerts, timers, or other overlays without capturing an ordinary browser window.

  1. Copy your overlay URL from Social Stream Ninja. Keep its session, password, and other options intact. Use the same session as your chat source.
  2. In OBS, choose Sources → + → Browser, name it, leave Local file off, and paste the full URL.
  3. Set Width and Height to the overlay’s intended size. For a full-frame landscape overlay, start with 1920 × 1080. This sets the web page’s layout area; dragging the source in OBS scales the result.
  4. For TTS or alerts with sound, enable Control audio via OBS. Adjust its mixer level and monitoring mode.
  5. Place the source above your camera/game in the Sources list. Send a test chat message or alert, then make a short recording.

Use Interact from the source’s right-click menu when you need to click inside a page. A Custom Browser Dock is an operator panel in the OBS interface; adding a dock alone does not put it on the video.

Browser source refresh, transparency, and shared audio

For persistent chat or timers, leave Shutdown source when not visible and Refresh browser when scene becomes active off initially. Shutdown unloads the page; refresh can reset its state. Use Refresh cache of current page manually when troubleshooting a stale page.

OBS’s default browser CSS allows transparency, but the overlay must also support a transparent background. Use the overlay’s own appearance options first. Keep page permissions at their default unless the overlay specifically needs OBS control.

Use Add Existing to share one browser source across scenes. Keep only one copy responsible for TTS or alert audio. See the SSN TTS guide, chat overlay quick start, and OBS Browser Source reference.

9. Make a recording, then go live

For a first local test, open Settings → Output → Recording, select a folder with free space, and choose High Quality, Medium File Size in Simple mode. Use a hardware encoder if available.

MKV is a resilient recording format if recording stops unexpectedly. Use File → Remux Recordings to make an MP4 for an editor without re-encoding. Current OBS also offers resilient Hybrid MP4/MOV; this differs from regular MP4/MOV. See OBS recording formats.

  1. Press Start Recording. Talk, play game/media sound, trigger an alert, and change scenes.
  2. Stop and play the file. Check framing, readable text, microphone clarity, lip sync, and whether alerts are too loud.
  3. Set your stream title, category, and visibility in the platform dashboard. Use a private or unlisted test if the service offers it; a normal Start Streaming action can make you publicly live.
  4. Press Start Streaming and check the platform preview and stream health. Some platforms also require a dashboard Go Live action. A good local recording does not test upload reliability.
  5. Finish with Stop Streaming, then confirm the event is ended in the platform dashboard. Stop a separate recording too if it is still running.
Optional: separate recording tracks and saved setups

In Advanced Output, choose the streaming audio track and recording tracks. A useful arrangement is track 1 for the full mix, track 2 for just the microphone, and track 3 for game audio. Assign sources in Advanced Audio Properties, then enable those tracks under Recording. Most ordinary players play one track at a time; separate tracks are for editing, so keep a full mix on track 1.

Profiles hold output and service settings. Scene Collections hold scenes and sources. Save a profile for a different platform and a scene collection for a different show; use their menus to export backups. More: OBS recording guide.

10. Fix the specific problem

Open View → Stats while testing. Network drops, rendering lag, and encoding lag have different causes.

  • Dropped frames (network): lower bitrate, stop competing uploads, and try wired Ethernet. Check the ingest server and platform status.
  • Frames missed due to rendering lag: leave room on the GPU. Cap the game’s frame rate, lower its graphics settings, and simplify animated scenes.
  • Skipped frames due to encoding lag: use a hardware encoder, a faster preset, or lower output resolution/FPS. A separate high-quality recording adds work too.
  • Black camera/game: check the selected device/window, OS permissions, the source’s eye icon, and whether another source covers it. Test a different capture method.
  • Silent stream with moving meters: check mute, Monitor Only, and the streaming track assignment. Headphone monitoring alone does not confirm output.
  • Double sound or echo: look for duplicate device capture, a monitored source returning through Desktop Audio, or an unmuted live player.
  • Blank SSN overlay: confirm chat reaches the SSN dock, compare session/password options, then refresh the Browser Source. A featured-message overlay may stay blank until you select a message.
